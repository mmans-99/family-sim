## The mistake to avoid first

You probably think of the world as *content*: a city, some jobs, a holiday scene, a recession event. That's a backdrop, and backdrops don't produce lives — they produce stages.

The world is a **set of clocks and ledgers**. Nothing in a life is scripted. Institutions have budgets, calendars, rules and exit costs; people have decaying needs and partial information; and a life is the interference pattern when all those frequencies collide. A recession is not an event you trigger — it's the aggregate of firms hiring less, which is the aggregate of revenue falling, which is people spending less because they're afraid of losing jobs. You build *mechanisms and dates*, and the holidays, funerals, promotions and evictions fall out on their own.

## The substrate: one engine, many domains

Everything a person encounters can be expressed as the same primitive, which is why you don't need fifteen systems:

> **stock** (money, trust, health, attention, space, reputation) + **inflows/outflows** with rates + **constraint** (a floor that can't be crossed) + **owner** + **ledger** (entry history, and what was promised vs. delivered) + **maturity** (when it comes due)

Money is a stock with a floor at zero. Sleep is a stock with a hard floor and a debt. A lease is a standing outflow with a maturity date. A marriage is a ledger with unspoken entries. A body is a stock with slow decline and sudden shocks. A house is a stock of space and condition. Once you build the stock/flow/constraint/maturity engine once, economy, household, health, and reputation are all instantiations.

Two extra properties matter and are usually missing: **lags** (every stock responds slowly) and **coupling** (touching one stock moves others — illness drains money and sleep and autonomy at once).

## Time, calendar, and ritual

Time is your most important object, and it must be **plural**:

- seconds — emotion, pain, impulse
- hours — energy, hunger, shifts
- days — mood, chores, news
- weeks — pay cycle, fatigue, social rhythm
- months — rent, seasons, school terms, pregnancy
- years — careers, children, health, cohort effects
- decades — housing, pensions, a body

The calendar is a real artifact: weekdays vs. weekends, paydays, term dates, tax year, lease renewals, school holidays, birthdays, anniversaries, electoral cycles, religious and national holidays, harvest, flu season.

Holidays should not be events. They are **dated obligations that generate expectations, costs, travel, gift ledgers, and family proximity**. That's why they're emotionally enormous: they force co-presence with people you have unresolved business with, at a fixed date, with a cultural script for how it "should" go — and the gap between the script and the actual household is where the feeling lives. Anniversaries are the mechanism by which the past reasserts itself: the same date re-triggers grief, guilt or nostalgia on a decay curve that never quite reaches zero, but spikes annually.

## Positions, not jobs

A job is not a property of a person. It's a **position in an org chart with a budget line**, and the person *occupies* it. This one change is what makes the world persist without your protagonist: when they quit, the position stays, gets refilled, and someone else's life changes.

Each position carries: wage and pay structure, contracted hours, **schedule rigidity** (this is the variable that quietly determines whether someone can be a parent, a carer, or a friend), autonomy, physical toll, status, commute, social density, supervisor relationship, and redundancy exposure.

The labour market has frictions and they're the point: incomplete information, credential gates, mobility costs, network dependence, discrimination, and **scarring**. Hiring and firing should emerge from revenue and headcount pressure, not from a script. Promotion is a *scarce slot* — someone's ascent requires someone else's stagnation.

## The economy as three loops, not a model

Don't build macro. Build:

1. **The income loop** — wages → spending → firm revenue → hiring/wages. Runs slow, has momentum.
2. **The credit loop** — interest rates set what's affordable; debt is a fixed claim on future income; asset prices (housing) respond to credit. This is where inequality actually lives: owners of assets vs. sellers of labour.
3. **The shock generator** — a process that emits disturbances of varying reach. What matters is **exposure**: sector, seniority, geography, tenure, savings buffer, whether your skills are specific to one employer.

The cardinal rule: **the person never experiences the economy, only changes in their constraints.** Nobody knows the unemployment rate. They know their brother got laid off, the shop on the corner shut, their hours were cut, and the landlord raised the rent. Information arrives through people and places, late and distorted.

## Housing and place

A home is not a location. It's a bundle: **tenure** (own / rent / social / with family / homeless), cost, space per person, condition, security of tenure, commute, and neighbourhood (safety, schools, social density, status, walkability).

Two things make this profound:

- **Exit costs are enormous and mostly social.** Moving destroys the weak ties that generate opportunity. Housing is the main mechanism by which people get stuck — not because they can't afford to leave, but because leaving costs the network.
- **Place determines who you can accidentally meet.** Your "life radius" — the set of places you frequent — generates your social world. Under scarcity and stress the radius contracts, which cuts off the very ties that could help. That feedback loop is one of the most realistic things you can simulate.

## Households, not individuals

This is the largest gap in what you have. **Finance is household finance.** Model:

- Pooled vs. separate accounts, and *who controls the account* — control is not proportional to contribution.
- **Unmeasured labour**: childcare, eldercare, cleaning, scheduling, remembering, absorbing other people's distress. Most domestic conflict is a dispute over the allocation of invisible work, and most simulations omit it entirely.
- **Children as agents**, not props. They have their own pressure systems, their needs ramp on a biological and social schedule, and they consume money, sleep, autonomy and relationship bandwidth in a curve you can plot. Their outcomes respond to household stress, parental attention and money in ways that are lagged by *years* — which is exactly what makes parenthood feel the way it does.
- **Elder care is the same curve inverted**, arriving in midlife, colliding with your children's adolescence and your career peak.

A household is a small economy with a production function, a governance structure, and a bargaining problem. Simulate the bargaining.

## The life course as a lattice

Life is a trajectory through **gated stages**: education → credential → entry job → partnering → children (with a real biological clock that doesn't care about your career clock) → peak earning → care obligations → decline → death. Each gate has admission requirements, a queue, and an exit cost.

The emotional engine here is **relative timing**. People evaluate their life against a reference group and an internal schedule. Being two years behind your cohort on housing, marriage or children produces more distress than absolute hardship does. And **cohort scarring** is real: the economy you entered in shapes your permanent wage trajectory, your risk appetite, and your politics.

## Institutions as autonomous agents

Bureaucracies, schools, banks, landlords, hospitals, insurers, welfare agencies, courts. They have rules, budgets, queues, targets and discretion — and employees with their own pressure systems and bad days.

Give them **failure modes**: eligibility cliffs, processing delays, lost paperwork, being disbelieved, rules applied literally by someone who doesn't care. Contact with institutions is one of the largest and most underrepresented sources of real suffering, and it's mechanically simple: it's a *filter with a probability of misclassification*, and that probability rises when the person's life is already chaotic — which is precisely when they need it to work.

## Health, body, death

Health is a stock with slow decline, punctuated by shocks, heavily correlated with stress, money, sleep and labour. Access is mediated by insurance, waiting lists, and whether you can afford to take a day off. Illness cascades: income falls, autonomy falls, relationships strain, the life radius contracts.

And death must be real. Parents die. Friends die. It should arrive on a mortality hazard, not a plot beat, and it should have a **long afterlife**: grief as a decaying process with anniversary spikes, the estate as a ledger to be settled, the house that has to be sold, the family that reconfigures around the absence.

## Culture, norms, and the invisible ledger

Norms are enforced by **disapproval costs** and they're local. Status is relative and domain-specific — you can be respected at church and contemptible at work. Class shows up as manners, vocabulary, taste, and *what you consider normal*, and it silently gates who you can partner with and which rooms you can enter.

Reputation lives at network level, not individual level: households have standing, families have histories, and information about you circulates through people who know people.

## Networks, chance, and the serendipity engine

The social graph is the **opportunity generator**: jobs, partners, housing, information. Weak ties matter more than strong ones for opportunity; strong ties matter for survival.

Encounters should be **rate-based, not scripted**: Poisson processes over shared places, weighted by routine overlap. The world tosses up candidates; the person's needs, attention and current mood decide which ones become real. This is how you get a life that feels both accidental and caused.

## The observation layer

The person never sees world state. They see what's visible from their locations, what their relationships tell them (distorted, late), and what media says (sensationalised, generic). Everyone — including you, the designer — should be able to say: *this agent is acting on stale, partial, emotionally biased information.* That's not a limitation, it's the source of realistic error and realistic tragedy.

## Design laws, if you only remember a few

1. **No scripts, only mechanisms and dates.** If you can name a scene in advance, it's content.
2. **The world must persist without the protagonist.** Institutions outlive people; positions get refilled.
3. **Everything has an entry cost and an exit cost**, and the exit is usually the expensive one.
4. **Scarcity narrows attention**, which narrows the life radius, which deepens scarcity.
5. **Everything is slower than you want.** No stock responds instantly; no relationship changes in a tick.
6. **The world should be able to ruin someone with no villain** — just tight coupling and bad timing.
7. **The world changes while they're not looking.** Off-screen life is not optional.

## How you know it works

Replay a life and check whether you get: an address history with plausible discontinuities; an income curve with jumps, gaps and plateaus; a social graph that grows, prunes and occasionally reactivates; health decline that tracks stress and money; and a set of turning points where **no single cause is sufficient but the conjunction is obvious in retrospect**. If a life can be summarised in one sentence of cause and effect, your world is still a stage. Real lives require three clauses to explain and still leave you unsure.
